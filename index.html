Create a resource

You do not need to arrive with a perfect prompt. Two paths are available: ask AI to help plan the resource, or use a ready-made prompt as a starting point. Both lead to a first draft that you review and improve.

1. Start with your teaching goal

Recommended when you are trying something new. Tell AI what students should learn, what you have taught, and what kind of thinking they must practice themselves. Ask it to suggest a useful approach and write the resource-generation prompt before creating anything.

You can also write a plain-language request: “I teach Grade 9 biology. Students keep confusing diffusion with osmosis. I have lesson notes and an exit ticket. Help me decide what practice would be useful, then write the prompt I should use.”

What to give AI

Use whichever sources genuinely define the goal, scope, student level, and accepted answers. An overview alone rarely captures what you actually taught.

  • Goals and scope: AP Course and Exam Description, departmental expectations, unit plans, learning objectives, or skill checklists.
  • Learning materials: Slides, readings, notes, textbook extracts you can share, demonstrations, primary sources, data, diagrams, or transcripts.
  • Examples and criteria: Nonsecure practice questions, worked solutions, exemplars, rubrics, accepted alternatives, and common misconceptions.
  • Student context: Grade, prerequisites, what students should do independently, feedback timing, and the format in which they will use the resource.

Work in drafts, not a single prompt

  1. Produce a small first draft rather than an entire unit.
  2. Inspect accuracy, coverage, difficulty, explanations, student independence, and usability.
  3. Tell AI exactly what to change, why, and which good parts to preserve. Repeat as needed.
  4. Check the revised output independently and, for QTI or app resources, test it in the actual tool.

Example revision request: “Questions 3 and 5 check the same idea. Question 6 needs a more plausible misconception. The feedback for question 2 gives away the next answer. Revise only those items, preserve the other questions and the learning targets, and explain what changed.”

Already have a prompt that produces disappointing results? Ask AI to critique and improve the prompt.

All resource types

Flashcards and spaced review

Use flashcards when students need to retrieve important knowledge again over time. A useful card can ask for a prediction, explanation, comparison, or correction, not only a definition.

Before you begin

  • Provide a learning target, current teaching materials, and any known misconceptions.
  • Ask for a manageable set; each card should have one clear retrieval target and a reason to revisit it.
  • Require the answer to remain hidden until the student has tried to retrieve or reason.

After AI responds

  • Check each answer independently and remove ambiguous or duplicate questions.
  • Confirm that each front is self-contained and asks students to do the intended thinking.
  • Decide when students should revisit the cards; simply producing cards does not make the practice spaced.

Do not accept the first set automatically. If cards duplicate one another, reward guessing, or omit an important idea, identify the exact cards and ask AI for targeted revisions. Then recheck the new answers.

Canvas practice questions

Draft multiple-choice questions that reveal plausible misconceptions and explain the reasoning behind each choice. Review the question quality first; generate a Canvas import package only after the content is approved.

Before you begin

  • Provide the learning targets, teaching materials, and examples of the expected difficulty.
  • Include mistakes or misconceptions students commonly make.
  • Eight questions with four choices is a practical starting size; adjust it to the learning target.

After AI responds

  • Solve each question yourself. Check that exactly one choice is defensibly correct.
  • Review the distractors, explanations, scope, and any code or calculations.
  • Keep teacher-only answers away from students until the planned feedback point.

If a distractor is implausible, a question has two defensible answers, or the feedback merely repeats the correct choice, tell AI which item failed and why. Regenerate those items before creating the QTI ZIP.

Extended-response practice and feedback

Create tasks that require students to explain, solve, or justify. Support more attempts with a clear rubric and a way to reconsider the work, without automatically supplying a finished answer.

Before you begin

  • Provide the learning goal, task format, and relevant lesson examples.
  • Include a rubric, exemplar, or description of what earns credit when available.
  • Decide what students should complete independently before they see feedback.

Rubric self-check: Students complete a response, compare it with criteria and an example, identify what is missing, and revise.

AI-supported feedback: Where permitted, students provide their own prior attempt and the rubric to an approved AI tool and receive response-specific formative critique. They should still evaluate and act on the feedback.

Give formative feedback on an attempt

Use the student-facing task and rubric, without handing over a teacher-only canonical solution.

Before using it with students

  • Test the rubric on a correct response, a plausible alternative, and a response with a specific mistake.
  • Compare AI feedback with teacher judgments before relying on it.
  • For real student work or student-facing AI services, follow the school policy and approval requirements.

If the task is too easy, the rubric rewards superficial features, or the sample response gives away the reasoning, tell AI exactly what needs correction. Revise the task and rubric together, then test them against more than one valid answer.

Review and improve

Creating useful materials is usually an iterative process: draft, inspect, give feedback, revise, and verify. One AI can support the entire cycle; a second can supply independent candidates or criticism.

Improve the prompt itself

If AI repeatedly creates questions that miss the level, target, or format, review the instructions and source materials before making another batch. This prompt asks AI to critique the prompt, not just polish its wording.

Improve an existing prompt

A useful follow-up after any draft: “These items have the right learning targets, but several explanations are vague and two answers are ambiguous. Fix the named items, preserve the stronger questions, and show what changed.”

Start with one AI

Have the model challenge its own questions or the resource it produced. Then make your own decisions using the original teaching materials and the student experience.

Check accuracy, ambiguity, and teaching quality

You can use this after a single-model draft or after combining candidates from several models.

Optional: My two-AI workflow

For larger resource sets, I often ask two AIs to draft candidates independently before either sees the other set. Then I give both sets to each AI, ask them to compare strengths and weaknesses, and curate a combined version. The resulting resource may go through further independent audits and classroom testing.

Two models can surface different ideas and errors, but agreement does not establish correctness. This approach also takes considerably more time than a quick one-prompt draft.

Before publishing or sharing

  • Verify answers against source materials, worked examples, calculations, or code execution where applicable.
  • Resolve disagreements with evidence, not by voting between AIs.
  • Check the actual student-facing interface or exported file. A plausible output is not a successful import or a reliable rubric.

Why I built these apps

Review and MCQs in one place

I wanted spaced review and practice MCQs together, with feedback immediately after each answer. Canvas, AP Classroom, and ordinary forms solved parts of that need, but not the whole workflow I wanted.

More frequent FRQ attempts

Students can be generous when scoring their own work, and teachers cannot personally mark every practice attempt. The AP CSA FRQ prototype copies a rubric-aware prompt for an external AI conversation. The Foundations review app instead provides a sample solution and rubric for self-checking.

These tools are teaching designs informed by learning research. They have not independently demonstrated improved learning outcomes.

The science of learning behind the tools

These principles can inform the design of flashcards, quizzes, and written-response practice even without building an app. They are intended as a starting point rather than an exhaustive account of learning.

Prior knowledge and working memory

Check prerequisites. Use clear instructions and worked examples when an idea is unfamiliar, then gradually remove supports.

Retrieval practice

Ask students to recall, explain, predict, or solve before revealing an answer. The best format depends on the learning target.

Spacing

Revisit important ideas across lessons or units. One flashcard session is not automatically spaced practice.

Feedback and misconceptions

Explain why a response works or why a tempting alternative fails. Give students a chance to act on the explanation.

Metacognition and self-assessment

Provide meaningful criteria for students to judge their own work, notice gaps, and decide what to revise.

Preserve meaningful effort, not unnecessary difficulty. Retrieval and problem solving can be effortful, but confusion is not an instructional goal. Useful support can make the intended thinking more accessible. Immediate feedback can be valuable for specific MCQ misconceptions, but timing depends on the task.

Research on student AI use

How students use generative AI matters. Faster work and higher-quality answers while AI is available do not automatically mean students can later retrieve, explain, or apply the same ideas independently. The growing evidence is mixed: some structured AI supports improve learning; other uses show little benefit or may replace valuable practice.

These are selected studies and syntheses, not a meta-analysis of everything published. Study populations, designs, and follow-up periods differ. Findings should not be generalized to every class or AI tool.

Broad reviews: where the evidence stands

  • Stanford SCALE: The Evidence Base on AI in K–12 (November 2026)Research synthesis, 52 causal papers. Student AI use has mixed effects, and much of the evidence is still postsecondary. Learning support looks more promising than handing over answers, but neither approach consistently produces learning gains. Long-term evidence remains limited.
  • OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026International evidence synthesis. AI-assisted performance and independent learning are different outcomes. Purposefully designed learning support and teacher-guided iteration show promise, but implementation matters.

Secondary-school evidence

These studies are closest to the age range of many colleagues’ classes. They report both encouraging and concerning results.

  • Bastani et al. (2025): AI without guardrails in high-school mathematicsPeer-reviewed randomized field experiment; nearly 1,000 high-school students in Türkiye. A general chatbot improved performance during practice but was associated with 17% lower scores on a later unassisted test. A guided tutor avoided that decline but did not demonstrate superior unassisted scores over the control group.
  • Kreijkes et al. (2026): Chatbot use and note-taking in secondary-school readingPeer-reviewed randomized experiment; 344 analyzed Year 10 students in England. Note-taking, with or without AI, supported better comprehension and retention than AI-only use three days later. Students often preferred the AI condition even when they learned less.
  • Fütterer et al. (2026): AI prompts for self-regulated learningPeer-reviewed randomized trial; 371 students in Grades 7–9 in Germany, in English and physics. Extra strategy or motivation prompts did not significantly outperform standard ChatGPT on tested subject knowledge or learning-strategy use. Some motivational differences appeared.
  • LearnLM Team, Google & Fab AI (2026): Guided Learning in Sierra LeonePreregistered, partner-reported randomized classroom trial; 1,763 junior-secondary mathematics students. Teacher-supported Gemini use across eight weeks improved endline math scores by approximately 0.26 standard deviations. This is a promising but setting-specific result with longer-term outcomes still unknown.
  • Strömberg, Lei & Wu (2026): The generative AI learning penaltyCEPR discussion paper; 26,811 students in Grades 7–12 in China. In a difference-in-differences analysis, AI adoption was associated with higher homework scores and shorter completion times but lower closed-book exam scores. This is nonrandomized evidence and should not be treated as proof that every type of AI use causes such losses.
  • Sungu, Lira & Duckworth (2026): Generative AI can harm teachingWorking-paper randomized school trial in Türkiye. An AI tool used mainly for lesson and exercise preparation reduced reported student motivation, with no overall achievement gain. Students of lower-performing teachers showed lower achievement and confidence. Teacher review and implementation remain important.

College and adult learning: useful comparisons

These studies extend the picture but do not directly establish what will happen with middle- or high-school students.

  • Kestin et al. (2025): A structured AI tutor in college physicsPeer-reviewed randomized crossover study; 194 undergraduates. A carefully scaffolded AI tutor produced larger immediate learning gains than an active-learning lesson for the material tested. It covered only two lessons, so it does not establish durable benefits or broad superiority.
  • Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting (2025): AI meets the programming classroomResearch preprint combining laboratory experiments and university field data. Overall experimental learning outcomes did not significantly differ; exploratory analyses suggested that asking for explanations was more beneficial than outsourcing exercise solutions. The behavior-dependent findings are not all established causal effects.
  • Shen & Tamkin (2026): How AI impacts skill formationResearch preprint; randomized study with 52 mostly junior adult developers learning an unfamiliar Python library. AI assistance reduced immediate conceptual, code-reading, and debugging performance on average without a significant speed advantage. Adult coding is not the same as general K–12 learning.

What to consider when designing AI-supported practice

Preserve the thinking students need to learn: attempting, retrieving, explaining, evaluating, and revising. Design feedback to identify gaps rather than merely improve the submitted product. Include a later opportunity to work independently, and judge the tool by what students can do without it, not only what they can produce with it.

Important qualification: independent attempts, guidance, and feedback timing are reasonable instructional choices, but the studies do not establish one universally best sequence. Some learners may benefit from direct explanations sooner. Monitor the results rather than treating any prompt or chatbot design as proven effective.
